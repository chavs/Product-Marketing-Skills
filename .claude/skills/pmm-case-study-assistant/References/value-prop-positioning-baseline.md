# Value Proposition, Positioning & Messaging Baseline

The foundational research track for any case study: establish what the target company itself currently says about its value proposition, positioning, and messaging — before doing any competitive analysis or content critique. Every other track reacts to this baseline; it has to be accurate and current, not a critique in its own right.

## Why this comes first

- A roadmap recommendation should extend or deliberately challenge the company's existing positioning, not accidentally contradict it out of ignorance.
- "Differentiation" from competitors only means something relative to what the company itself claims to stand for.
- A content critique needs to know the *intended* message before judging whether a given page actually delivers it.

## Cover every capability/use-case area the brief touches, not just the newest one

A company extending into a new area (a new product line, a new use-case category) often runs two messaging threads at once — the established one and the new one — and how they relate to each other is itself a finding. Research each area named in the brief side by side, and explicitly note:

- Does the newer area extend the same core narrative, or does it read as a separate, bolted-on story?
- Is the terminology consistent across areas (same category language, same proof-point style), or does the newest material use different vocabulary than the established pages?
- Do the areas share an ICP/buyer, or is the newer area aimed at a different buyer/persona than the established one?

## What to capture, per area

1. **Core value proposition statement(s)** — as close to verbatim as the source material states it, not a paraphrase.
2. **Primary ICP/buyer language** — who they say it's for, in their own words.
3. **Category framing** — what market/problem category they position themselves in, especially whether that framing has shifted recently.
4. **Top proof points/claims** — the specific evidence (customer counts, benchmark numbers, named integrations, analyst mentions) they repeat most across pages.
5. **Messaging inconsistencies across pages** — flag them, don't silently reconcile. An inconsistency between an older product page and a newer one is a finding worth surfacing, not a discrepancy to paper over.

## Source-trust ranking

This track is specifically about what the company *itself* says, so its own materials are the highest-trust source here — unlike a competitive-fact track, there's no concern about vendor bias, because the vendor's own voice is exactly what's being researched.

1. The company's own product/solution pages, solution briefs, and technical documentation
2. The company's own press releases, investor materials, and executive statements (corroborating or extending what the product pages say)
3. Practitioner or analyst commentary *about* the company's positioning — useful for gap-checking (does outside perception match stated positioning), not as the primary source for what the company itself claims

## Tagging

Same discipline as every other track: `[Confirmed]` / `[Estimated]` / `[Inferred]`, inline source link on every claim, no exceptions.
